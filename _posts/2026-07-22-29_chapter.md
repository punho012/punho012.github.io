## Phile 29. Levels of OPSEC

In the previous chapter, we built our threat model by identifying our assets, adversaries, and risks. The next step is choosing the appropriate level of protection.

There is no universally accepted classification for OPSEC levels, but I find the following model useful as a practical way to think about everyday operational security. The stronger your threat model, the higher the level of protection you should adopt.

For simplicity, we will distinguish four levels:

Level 1 – Public Use
Level 2 – Private Use
Level 3 – Anonymous Use
Level 4 – Sensitive Use

We will compare these levels using five key characteristics:

Transparency – Do you understand what the software is doing?
Surveillance Resistance – How well does it protect you from tracking and monitoring?
Centralization – Who controls the service and your data?
Anonymity – Can your identity be linked to your online activities?
Deniability – Can you plausibly deny performing a particular action?


### Level 1 – Public Use

This level is intended for everyday online activities where privacy is not the primary concern. Your real identity is used, and your actions are generally expected to be associated with you.

Typical examples include:

- Online banking
- Healthcare portals
- Insurance providers
- Government services

For these services, anonymity is neither practical nor desirable. Instead, security should be the highest priority by using strong passwords, multi-factor authentication (MFA), and trusted devices.


### Level 2 – Private Use
This level focuses on protecting your privacy while maintaining a persistent online identity through a pseudonym.

You are not anonymous, but you minimize unnecessary data collection and online tracking.

- Transparency: Prefer open-source software whenever possible.
- Surveillance Resistance: Use privacy-enhancing technologies such as VPNs, encrypted messaging, and privacy-respecting browsers.
- Centralization: Centralized services are acceptable, although decentralized alternatives are preferable when practical.
- Anonymity: You operate under a pseudonym rather than your real identity.
- Deniability: Activities remain associated with your pseudonym, so plausible deniability is generally limited.

### Level 3 – Anonymous Use
At this level, protecting your identity becomes the primary objective.

Activities should not be linkable to your real identity or to each other. This often requires compartmentalization, anonymous identities, and anonymity networks.

- Transparency: Open-source software is strongly preferred.
- Surveillance Resistance: Use multiple privacy-preserving technologies such as Tor, I2P, VPNs, and hardened browsers where appropriate.
- Centralization: Minimize reliance on centralized services whenever possible.
- Anonymity: Your online identity should not be attributable to your real identity.
- Deniability: Depending on the circumstances, attribution becomes significantly more difficult, although no technology can guarantee perfect deniability.

### Level 4 – Sensitive Use
This level is intended for situations where exposure could have severe legal, financial, or physical consequences.

Examples include investigative journalism, work with confidential sources, whistleblower communications, or intelligence collection in hostile environments.

At this level, operational security extends beyond online anonymity and includes protecting data against physical compromise.

- Transparency: Use trustworthy, preferably open-source software.
- Surveillance Resistance: Employ layered privacy protections and minimize metadata exposure.
- Centralization: Prefer self-hosted or decentralized services whenever feasible.
- Anonymity: Combine anonymity networks with strong compartmentalization practices.
- Deniability: Full-disk encryption, compartmentalized identities, secure device handling, and proper operational procedures improve plausible deniability, although no solution is absolute.

## Identities

One of the most important principles of OPSEC is identity separation.

Each use case should have its own independent identity that cannot be linked to your other activities. Never reuse usernames, email addresses, phone numbers, profile pictures, writing styles, or other identifiable information across different identities.

Anonymous identities are particularly difficult to maintain over long periods. They should be treated as disposable or short-lived whenever possible and rotated regularly to reduce the risk of correlation.

## Final takeaway

Not everyone needs the same level of operational security. Effective OPSEC is about applying the right level of protection to the right situation rather than maximizing privacy at all costs. Your threat model determines the safeguards you need, and your operational practices determine whether those safeguards remain effective.
