## Phile 31 - Mass Surveillance — When Everyone Becomes a Target
Surveillance is the systematic observation and monitoring of a person, population, or location, with the purpose of information-gathering, influencing, managing, or directing. Surveillance can take many forms, from physical observations and CCTV cameras to digital monitoring and communication interception.
Digital surveillance in the modern digital age has become easier to scale. Modern technologies can collect and process information about millions of peopleat the same time.

## Online surveillance

Today, we live in a highly interconnected digital world. We use social media, mobile applications, online shopping platforms, navigation services, and countless other digital services every day.

Visiting websites, downloading files, logging into accounts, watching videos, using mobile applications, and communicating with others can all generate data that may be collected and analyzed by organizations with the appropriate access.

This does not necessarily mean that someone is actively watching every individual. Much of modern surveillance is automated, relying on large-scale data collection, analytics, and profiling.

Two useful ways to understand online surveillance are mass surveillance and targeted surveillance.

### Mass surveillance

Mass surveillance refers to the collection, processing, or analysis of information about large numbers of people without limiting the activity to individuals who are specifically suspected of wrongdoing. It can involve communications data, location information, browsing activity, facial recognition, or other forms of digital information.

Mass surveillance can be conducted by governments, intelligence agencies, law enforcement organizations, and, in a different context, commercial companies that collect large amounts of personal data.

One important example is surveillance capitalism, a term commonly used to describe business models built around the extensive collection and analysis of personal data for commercial purposes. Large technology and advertising companies can use collected information to personalize advertisements, analyze behavior, and build detailed user profiles. Data can also move through complex ecosystems involving advertisers, analytics providers, data brokers, and other third parties.

Data brokers are another important part of this ecosystem. They can collect information from multiple sources, combine datasets, create profiles, and provide information to other organizations. This means that data collected by different services can potentially be correlated even when those services do not directly share information with each other.

Governments can also conduct large-scale surveillance for purposes such as intelligence gathering, law enforcement, national security, or public safety. In some cases, governments may obtain information directly from telecommunications providers, technology companies, or other data holders. The legal frameworks and safeguards surrounding these practices vary significantly between jurisdictions.

### Targeted Surveillance

Targeted surveillance is directed toward specific individuals, groups, or organizations. Instead of collecting information indiscriminately from a large population, the surveillance focuses on particular targets based on an investigation, intelligence requirement, or other defined objective.

Highly capable threat actors, including governments and intelligence services, may have access to sophisticated surveillance capabilities. Depending on the circumstances, targeted surveillance can involve communications interception, device exploitation, spyware, location tracking, or other techniques.

This distinction is important from an OPSEC perspective. A person does not need to be a high-value target to be affected by mass surveillance, while targeted surveillance usually requires a specific reason or interest in the individual.

## Why Does This Matter for OPSEC?

Mass surveillance changes the way we should think about privacy. You do not have to be specifically targeted for your data to become part of a large dataset.

This is why OPSEC is not only about hiding your identity from a specific adversary. It is also about minimizing unnecessary data exposure and reducing the number of signals that can be collected, correlated, and used to build a profile about you.

The goal is not necessarily to become invisible. In most situations, that would be unrealistic. The goal is to reduce unnecessary exposure and make unwanted correlation more difficult.

## Final Takeaway

Mass surveillance demonstrates why privacy cannot be reduced to protecting the content of our communications. Large-scale data collection, metadata, and correlation can reveal information about individuals even when they are not specifically targeted. Understanding this threat is an important part of OPSEC because it changes the question from "Am I being watched?" to "How much information am I leaving behind, and who can potentially correlate it?"
