### What is threat modeling?

Threat modeling is the process of identifying what you want to protect, who do you want to protect from, how those assets could be attacked and what consequences would be if those attacks succeeded.
There is no universal threat models that applies to everyone. Every individual faces different risks depending on their profession, location, activities and information they own.
The actions you should take will be very different if you are a journalist working with whistleblowers, a political activist, a government employee, a business executive, or simply an ordinary interent user who lives in a democratic country. Threat modeling helps to determine which security and privacy measures are appropriate for your specific situation.
For example, investigative journalists handling sensitive sources require much higher level of privacy because they possess valuable information and are far more likely to become targets of surveillance or attacks. An average internet user may face a lower risk of targeted attacks, but they are still exposed to cybercriminals, phishing campaigns, identitiy theft, data breaches, and mass surveilance. 
Threat modeling is the first step toward improving privacy and operational security. Before choosing any tools, first you need to understand what you are protecting, who your adversaries are, and what the potential consequences of failure might be.

### Building your threat model

The following questions form of the foundation of every threat model:

1. **Assets**
**What do you want to protect?** 
Assets can be physical such as laptop, documents, mobile phones as well as non-physical including personal data, bank account, email account, intellectual property, financial information, passwords.

2. **Adversaries**
**Who do you want to protect your assets from?**
The adversary is any individual or group capable of targeting you or your assets. Their motivations, capabilities and available resources vary significantly.
Potential adversaries include cybercriminals, nation-state actors, governments, stalkers, rogue employee, former partner, business competition.
The defensive measure required against a nation-state actor are obviously defferent from those needed against an online scammer.

3. **Consequences**
**What happens if you fail?**
Evaluate the potential impact if one of your assets is compromised. Would it result in financial loss, reputational damage, identity theft, legal consequences, or even physical harm? 

4. **Likelyhood**
**How likely is the threat?**
Not every threat deserves the same level of attention. Consider how likely is that a particular adversary will target you. This helps prioritize security measures and avoid unnecessary complexity.

### Attack Surface

Another important concept closely related to threat modeling is the attack surface.
Attack surface is the collection of possible entry points through which an attacker could compromise your systems or data.
Every file you download, account you create, application you install increases your attack surface.
An attack vector is one individual path that an attacker can exploit. The attack surface is simply the sum of all those possible attack vectors.
One of the primary goals of OPSEC is to minimize your attack surface. Fewer accounts, fewer applications generally means fewer opportunities for attackers.

### Final takeaway

Threat modeling is the foundation of effective operational security. Before choosing privacy tools or implementing defensive measures, you must first understand what you are protecting, who your adversaries are, and how realistic the threats actually are. Good security is not about using every available tool—it is about making informed decisions based on your own risks.
